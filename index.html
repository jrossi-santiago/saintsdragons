One night, end to end

Not three things. One thing that comes back around.

This is Monday 21 September, exactly as it was sent — nothing here is a mock-up. The history and the tale are not two items on a list. They are the same idea, told twice: once to you, once to them. Then it carries into tomorrow.

  1. 01

    After work · five minutes · yours

    You read the history

    The Battle of Hastings, 1066. Harold Godwinson drove his household troops 185 miles in five days, destroyed a Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge, then marched the length of England again and stood in the shield wall himself. Two ways of making war met on a ridge and neither could undo the other, so it ran from nine in the morning until dusk.

    Written like a man talking to you, not like a textbook. You finish it knowing something you did not know at breakfast.

  2. 02

    Bedtime · two minutes · read aloud

    They hear the same thing as a story

    The Lion and the Mouse. A lion laughs at a mouse who promises to help him one day. The day comes, the net holds him, and the small one everybody counted out chews him free.

    That pairing is the whole product. The card says why the two go together in one line: “Harold marched the length of England twice in three weeks and still stood in the shield wall himself. We don't get to sit out the fight because we're tired — we show up anyway, and that is most of what courage actually is.”

  3. 03

    Tomorrow · at the table

    You have something to ask

    Every card carries one question, written for the child who heard the story, not for the adult who read the history. You don't have to think of it at 7am.

    Ask them

    “The mouse promised to help a lion, and the lion almost laughed at her. Has anyone ever been surprised that you could help them?”

  4. 04

    Before lights out · one line

    And something to close it

    Four or five seconds in the dark. Say it or don't — it is printed at the bottom of every card, and it is the reason this is a tradition and not a trivia feed.

    Before lights out

    “For steady hands when the ground gives way beneath us, and for the small kindnesses that come back around exactly when we need them.”

Then it starts again. Tomorrow there is a new date, a new pair, a new question, and the same shape — so the habit is the thing that carries, not your memory of what you meant to teach them.
